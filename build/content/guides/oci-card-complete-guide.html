The Overseas Citizen of India scheme has existed since 2005, and for most of that time it worked the same way: an application form, a stack of photocopies, a long wait, and a blue booklet stamped into your passport. In 2026 that model ended. If you hold an OCI, are applying for one, or have children who might need one, these are the changes that matter.

1. The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2026

The amended rules came into force on 30 April 2026 and rewrote the OCI process around an online system:

  • Applications, renewals and renunciations are filed online on ociservices.gov.in, with one set of scanned documents instead of duplicate paper copies.
  • The OCI register is electronic, and an OCI can exist as a physical card or an electronic registration.
  • There is now a formal revision route if a mission, post or FRRO refuses or cancels an OCI, and applicants must get a reasonable opportunity to present their case.
  • Fingerprints become a choice point — the form asks whether biometrics may be used for automatic enrolment in fast-track immigration.
  • Renouncing an OCI is an online declaration followed by handing back any physical card, with a digital acknowledgement.

2. The e-OCI

The electronic OCI launched at the end of June 2026. Mission notices published in July state that from 8 July 2026 only digital e-OCIs are issued to new applicants, and that you present the e-OCI on your phone together with your passport at immigration and to participating airlines.

If you already hold a physical OCI card, it remains valid. You don’t need to swap it, though you can store a digital copy as well.

Practical tip: keep an offline copy (screenshot or PDF) of your e-OCI, and carry the passport it is linked to. Airline check-in staff are still adjusting to digital OCIs; a printed copy is a cheap insurance policy.

3. The three-month passport update window

This is the rule most cardholders meet first. Every time you get a new foreign passport, you must update your OCI record with the new passport details and a recent photo. Within three months it’s free. After three months, a late fee of US$25 applies, plus a small Indian Community Welfare Fund charge in some places.

Travel publications reported in November 2026 that this late fee is being waived from 1 November to 31 December 2026, and that the waiver does not apply to late applications already submitted before 1 November. If you’ve been putting off an update, the end of 2026 is the moment to do it — but confirm the waiver on the OCI portal before relying on it.

4. Children and two passports

The 2026 rules state plainly that a minor cannot hold an Indian passport and a foreign passport at the same time. This was always the intent — parents used to sign a declaration — but it is now an explicit rule. It matters most for children born in countries that grant citizenship by birth, such as the US and Canada.

If your child holds a foreign passport, the usual route is an OCI, not an Indian passport. If your child holds an Indian passport and you are considering another nationality, plan the sequence with your mission first. Read our guide to OCI for children born abroad.

5. How you submit has changed too

The online rules sit alongside local changes in how missions take in applications. Several US missions discontinued postal OCI applications from April 2026, so a visit to the VFS centre is now part of the process there; in Canada, OCI applicants were moved to in-person submission with biometrics at BLS centres from April 2026. Travelling to a centre is now a real cost to plan for, especially if you live far from one.

What hasn’t changed

The eligibility grounds — Indian origin up to great-grandparents, or marriage of two years or more to an Indian citizen or OCI cardholder — and the main exclusions are the same. The benefits are the same: lifelong multiple-entry travel and no FRRO registration however long you stay. And the restrictions are the same: no voting, no agricultural land, and special permission for protected areas and certain activities such as research and journalism.
